Spain still runs one of the best remote-work residency programmes in Europe, and Americans still make up a large share of the queue. But the programme you read about in 2023 no longer matches the programme you will file in 2026. The income bar moved, the Digital Nomad Office rebuilt its review team, one popular shortcut disappeared, and Spain rewrote the renewal checklist this month. This guide covers the current rules and the parts that specifically bite US citizens.

## What changed in 2026

Four developments matter if you plan to file this year.

**The income threshold rose.** On 17 February 2026 the Council of Ministers approved Royal Decree 126/2026, lifting Spain's minimum interprofessional wage (SMI) by 3.1% to €1,221 per month across 14 payments — €17,094 per year. Because the visa pegs its economic-means test to the SMI, the single-applicant threshold climbed to €2,849 per month, up from €2,763 in 2025.

**The Digital Nomad Office restructured.** The UGE-CE (*Unidad de Grandes Empresas y Colectivos Estratégicos*) staffed a more senior, specialised review team in early 2026 and created a Quality Control Unit. That unit verifies whether approved applicants actually registered with Spanish Social Security afterwards. Two problems drove the tightening: forged employment contracts and non-existent companies on one side, and approved nomads who never registered with Social Security on the other. The office also announced that fraud found in one file can trigger a review of every other file submitted by the same representative — so choose your gestor or lawyer carefully.

**The in-country switch from the non-lucrative visa closed.** Plenty of Americans landed on a [non-lucrative visa]({{ '/guides/non-lucrative-visa-spain/' | relative_url }}), waited a year, then converted to digital nomad status without leaving Spain. The UGE-CE confirmed in 2026 that it no longer permits that modification. NLV holders who want to work remotely must now exit Spain and apply through a consulate.

**Renewals got a new document list in July 2026.** Spain stripped out the diploma, the CV, the employer letter, and the proof of three years' experience — and added copies of your Spanish tax returns. More on that below.

## Who qualifies as an American applicant

Spain designed the *visado y autorización de residencia para teletrabajo de carácter internacional* for non-EU nationals who work through computers and telecommunications for companies or clients outside Spain. US citizens and green card holders qualify on nationality alone. You then need to clear five gates:

- **A qualifying work relationship.** You either work as an employee of a non-Spanish company, or you freelance for a client base that sits mainly outside Spain.
- **Seniority.** You have worked with that employer or those clients for at least three months before you file, and the company itself has operated for at least one year.
- **Professional credibility.** You hold a university or postgraduate degree, or you document at least three years of relevant professional experience. Apostille the degree if you use that route.
- **Income.** You meet the 2026 threshold below, before tax.
- **The 80/20 rule.** Spanish-sourced income stays at or below 20% of your total professional activity, from day one and at every renewal.

Two practical notes for Americans. First, W-2 employees do get approved — the UGE-CE has cleared salaried US workers whose employers formally authorise remote work from Spain, though reviewers assess each case individually and documentation quality decides the outcome. Second, the UGE-CE now reads job titles closely. Your title and job description must describe a 100% remote role. Anything implying on-site presence, physical operations, or production oversight invites a refusal. Applying as "Owner" or "Director" of your own company creates similar problems; specialists generally restructure those applicants as employees or contractors of the company instead.

## 2026 income thresholds

Spain calculates the requirement from the annual SMI (€17,094), averaged across 12 months:

| Applicant | Monthly (gross) | Annual (gross) |
|---|---|---|
| Main applicant (200% SMI) | €2,849 | €34,188 |
| First family member (+75% SMI) | +€1,068 | +€12,821 |
| Each additional family member (+25% SMI) | +€356 | +€4,274 |
| Couple | ≈ €3,917 | ≈ €47,009 |
| Couple with one child | ≈ €4,273 | ≈ €51,283 |

Show the money in dollars and you invite exchange-rate arguments, so build a cushion. Practitioners commonly advise documenting at least €3,000 per month for a single applicant. If your earnings fall slightly short, you may top up the gap with savings, but you should treat that as a repair strategy rather than a plan.

Reviewers care about consistency more than peak numbers. Assemble three to six months of payslips or invoices, the contracts behind them, and bank statements showing those exact amounts landing in your account. Mismatches between payroll and deposits slow a 20-day decision more reliably than anything else.

> **Verify before you file.** The threshold moves whenever Spain revises the SMI, usually in January or February, and individual consulates publish their own rounding. Confirm the current figure against the BOE or your consulate's checklist on the day you submit.

## Two routes: consulate or UGE-CE

Americans choose between two genuinely different processes.

**Route 1 — the Spanish consulate in the US.** You book an appointment at the consulate covering your state of legal residence, submit in person, and surrender your passport during processing. Spain's mobility-visa law sets a 10-business-day decision window, though real timelines stretch to 15–45 days depending on the post. Approval gives you a **one-year visa**. You then travel to Spain and convert it into the three-year residence authorisation. Freelancers usually register as autónomo *before* the consulate issues the visa.

**Route 2 — the UGE-CE from inside Spain.** Americans enter Spain visa-free, and the law lets you file the residence application electronically while your 90-day stay remains legal. This route delivers a **three-year residence authorisation immediately**, resolves in about 20 business days, and defers autónomo registration until after approval. You will need a *certificado digital* to file through the Mercurio portal, or a representative who holds one, and you should submit at least 30 days before your tourist stay expires.

Most specialists now steer Americans toward Route 2 when the timing works. You skip the consulate queue, you get three years instead of one, and you avoid committing to a tax structure before anyone approves it. Route 1 still suits applicants who need certainty before they sell a house, give notice, or move children.

One warning that trips people up: entering Spain as a tourist to file a fresh digital nomad application remains legal. Converting an existing non-work permit into digital nomad residency from inside Spain does not. Those are two different things, and only the second one closed.

## The American document stack

Spain asks every applicant for broadly the same evidence. The friction for Americans sits almost entirely in *how* you produce it.

**The criminal record certificate.** Spain accepts only the federal-level check — the **FBI Identity History Summary**. A state police certificate fails, however official it looks. Order the summary (fingerprint submission, then a few working days for the result), then apostille it at the **US Department of State**, not at a state Secretary of State. Budget six to eight weeks end to end, and longer if the State Department backs up. The certificate must cover the past two years and generally stays valid for three to six months, so sequence it against your appointment rather than ordering it first.

**Other apostilles.** State-issued documents — birth certificates, marriage certificates — go to the relevant state's Secretary of State. Federal documents go to the State Department. Mix those up and you restart the clock.

**Sworn translations.** A *traductor jurado* recognised by Spain's Ministry of Foreign Affairs must translate every foreign-language document. A bilingual friend, a notary, or a general translation agency will not clear the bar.

**Private health insurance.** Buy a policy from an insurer authorised to operate in Spain (DGSFP-registered), with full coverage equivalent to the public system, **zero co-payments and zero deductibles**. Travel insurance fails. So does a US employer plan with international coverage. Non-compliant insurance ranks among the most common refusal grounds, and it is also the easiest to avoid.

**Employer evidence.** Your employer supplies a letter authorising remote work from Spain, confirming the relationship has run at least three months, and evidencing at least 12 months of company operation. The UGE-CE increasingly wants company registration documents apostilled too. The wording of the authorisation letter carries real weight — have someone who has read a hundred of them read yours.

**Income and identity.** Passport valid at least one year with two blank pages, the application form, biometric photos, contracts, payslips or invoices, bank statements, and family documents where relevant.

## The Certificate of Coverage problem

This single issue derails more American applications than income, insurance, and criminal records combined.

Article 74 ter of Ley 14/2013 requires applicants to show they comply with social security obligations. Spanish consulates and the UGE-CE apply that rule two ways: either you register with Spanish Social Security, or you prove a bilateral agreement covers you elsewhere. For Americans, the relevant instrument is the **US-Spain Totalization Agreement**, in force since 1 April 1988, and the evidence is a **Certificate of Coverage** from the Social Security Administration.

Several things about that certificate surprise people:

- **The employer requests it, not the employee.** The SSA's Office of International Programs handles it, through the online portal at opts.ssa.gov or by mail or fax.
- **It is not Form SSA-1945.** That form serves an unrelated domestic purpose. Anyone who tells you to file SSA-1945 for your visa has confused two documents.
- **The SSA publishes no standard turnaround.** Practitioners routinely report waits of several months.
- **The agreement was not written for you.** Its detached-worker rule contemplates an employer sending someone to Spain temporarily. An employee who decides independently to move and keep the same job sits in an awkward middle space. The SSA declined many such requests historically, and while practitioners report more approvals from 2024 onward, no published SSA guidance names digital nomad scenarios. Spanish practitioners also report that the UGE-CE now looks for a letter of displacement alongside the certificate for US files.

Plan for refusal as well as approval. If the SSA says no, three fallbacks exist. Your employer registers directly with Spain's Tesorería General de la Seguridad Social as a foreign employer — heavy, but workable, and a payroll provider can run the mechanics. You convert to a 1099 contractor and register as autónomo in Spain. Or you restructure entirely. Note one trap: a standard Employer of Record arrangement, where a Spanish entity becomes your legal employer, **disqualifies you**, because the visa requires your employment relationship to sit with a non-Spanish company.

Self-employed Americans face a cleaner rule and a harder outcome. The agreement covers self-employed workers on a residence basis — live in Spain, contribute in Spain. Once you pass 183 days and become tax resident, RETA registration becomes the natural result. Some freelancers obtain a certificate under the "temporary transfer of trade" exception, but the SSA scrutinises those claims closely.

Start this process three to four months before your consulate appointment. Not three weeks.

## Spanish tax: the Beckham decision

Spend more than 183 days in Spain in a calendar year and Spain treats you as a tax resident on worldwide income, at progressive IRPF rates that run roughly 19% to 47% depending on your autonomous community.

The alternative is Article 93 LIRPF — the **Beckham regime**. It taxes qualifying employment income at a flat **24% up to €600,000**, and 47% above that, for the year you arrive plus the following five tax years. Foreign passive income (dividends, capital gains, most rents) generally escapes Spanish tax during that window, and you skip Modelo 720 asset reporting.

Three hard truths about Beckham for Americans:

1. **The visa and the regime are separate decisions by separate authorities.** The UGE-CE grants residency. The Agencia Tributaria decides Beckham, on **Modelo 149**, within **six months of your Spanish Social Security registration**. That deadline does not extend, and the tax office has tightened its scrutiny of both the deadline and the substance of the underlying employment relationship.
2. **Freelancers do not qualify.** Spain's Directorate-General for Taxes has consistently excluded ordinary autónomo, freelance, and consulting income from Article 93. Narrow carve-outs exist for ENISA-certified startup founders and for highly qualified professionals doing R&D or innovation work for Spanish entities, but they catch very few US nomads.
3. **Beckham does not always win.** Below roughly €35,000–€40,000 of income, the flat 24% can cost more than progressive rates, because you surrender personal deductions and some treaty benefits.

Plan your budget on standard IRPF and treat Beckham as upside. If your numbers only work with Beckham, pay a Spanish tax specialist for a written opinion before you sign a lease.

## US tax: what follows you across the Atlantic

Moving to Spain adds a tax system; it does not replace one.

**You keep filing.** The US taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residence. The US-Spain tax treaty (in force since 1990, updated by a protocol effective 27 November 2019) allocates taxing rights and provides credit mechanisms, but its saving clause preserves America's right to tax its own citizens more or less as though the treaty did not exist.

**Foreign Earned Income Exclusion.** For tax year 2026 the FEIE reaches **$132,900** per qualifying person (up from $130,000 in 2025), claimed on Form 2555. You qualify through the bona fide residence test or the physical presence test — 330 full days outside the US in any 12-month period. The 2026 base housing amount sits at $21,264 and the general housing-expense cap at $39,870, before location-specific increases.

**Foreign Tax Credit.** Form 1116 credits Spanish tax against US liability. Higher earners frequently do better with the credit than the exclusion, particularly once Spanish rates exceed US rates. Combining both requires care, since you cannot claim a credit for tax paid on excluded income.

**FBAR.** File FinCEN Form 114 when your foreign accounts, added together, exceed **$10,000** at any moment in the year. That includes an ordinary Spanish current account holding your rent money — one more reason to read our guide to [opening a bank account in Spain]({{ '/guides/opening-a-bank-account-spain/' | relative_url }}) before you arrive.

**FATCA.** File Form 8938 once you cross the thresholds for filers abroad: more than $200,000 on the last day of the year or $300,000 at any point (single or married filing separately), and $400,000 / $600,000 filing jointly.

**Modelo 720.** Spain requires residents to declare foreign assets above €50,000 per category — unless Beckham shields you during its six-year window.

Two more items Americans routinely forget: your state may keep claiming you as a resident until you cut specific ties, and US retirement and investment accounts behave unpredictably under Spanish rules once Beckham lapses. Bring a US-licensed CPA and a Spanish advisor into the same conversation. Neither one sees the whole board alone.

## Costs and realistic timeline

Government fees stay modest. The UGE-CE application runs about €73 per person, the renewal about €79, and the TIE card about €16. Consular fees land between €80 and €100 depending on reciprocity.

The real money goes elsewhere:

- FBI Identity History Summary and federal apostille: roughly $80–$200, plus expediting services if you use one
- Apostilles on other documents: €20–€60 each
- Sworn translations: €30–€100 per document
- Compliant Spanish health insurance: €60–€150 per month per person
- Immigration lawyer or specialist gestor: variable, and increasingly worth it given the 2026 enforcement climate

A self-managed single application typically totals €300–€700 in hard costs before professional fees.

On timing, work backwards from your target arrival. Document gathering takes six to ten weeks, dominated by the FBI check and its apostille. The Certificate of Coverage takes three to four months when it works. The decision itself takes 10–45 days at a consulate or about 20 business days at the UGE-CE — where 20 business days of silence counts as approval, though you still need paperwork in hand for the TIE. After approval in Spain, register your *empadronamiento* and book biometrics; TIE appointments have become genuinely hard to secure in several regions, so start hunting the day you get approved.

## Renewal rules changed in July 2026

Spain published a lighter renewal list this month — and a heavier substantive test.

Spain **removed** the diploma and education proof (and its apostilles), the CV, the company registration certificate, the employer letter authorising remote work, bank statements and invoices, and the proof of three years' experience. The logic is sensible: your first three-year permit already demonstrated the experience.

Spain **now asks** for:

1. A social security coverage certificate (employees) or *vida laboral* (autónomos)
2. Your passport data page, current — show the newest one if you replaced it
3. The fee and payment receipt
4. The MIT form, preferably digitally signed
5. Your valid contract or contracts
6. **Copies of tax returns for two periods filed with the Spanish tax office (AEAT)** — Spanish filings only, not American ones. If you lack two annual returns, submit what exists plus recent quarterly filings.
7. Private health insurance (employees only)

Alongside the shorter list, the UGE-CE tests things it previously took on trust. You must show you actually lived in Spain — at least six months per year — and that your income and work situation still meet the *current* threshold, not the one that applied when you first qualified. Someone who cleared €2,646 in 2024 must now clear €2,849. The 20% Spanish-income cap gets checked retrospectively, and files well above it face refusal.

A lighter list does not mean an easier decision. Reviewers may still request anything not on it — notarised bank statements have appeared repeatedly — so keep the full set ready. Autónomos renewing with a different client from the one declared originally should prepare extra explanation.

The permit renews in two-year blocks up to five years total. At five years of lawful residence you may apply for long-term residency; those years also count toward naturalisation, which most Americans reach at ten years.

## Why the UGE-CE refuses American files

Patterns from 2026 refusals, in rough order of frequency:

- **Health insurance with co-payments** or from an insurer not authorised in Spain
- **A job description that implies physical presence** rather than fully remote work
- **Missing or wrongly issued social security evidence** — no Certificate of Coverage, no Spanish registration, or a certificate whose wording reads as an intracompany transfer
- **A state background check instead of the FBI summary**, or the apostille obtained from the wrong authority
- **Income documented in a way reviewers cannot reconcile** — contracts saying one thing, deposits saying another
- **Applying as owner or director** of your own company
- **Failing to register with Spanish Social Security after approval**, which the Quality Control Unit now checks directly

Every one of these is preventable before you file. None of them is easy to fix afterwards.

## Your 90-day planning window

One more piece of context shapes the in-country route. The EU's Entry/Exit System became fully operational on 10 April 2026, and it now records your Schengen entries and exits biometrically instead of by passport stamp. Border officers no longer squint at ink; the system counts your 90 days in 180 automatically and precisely. If you plan a scouting trip and then a separate return to file with the UGE-CE, count those days carefully — the margin for error disappeared.

ETIAS, the separate pre-travel authorisation, is **not** live. The EU removed its "last quarter of 2026" target in mid-July 2026 and has published no replacement date, so Americans currently travel to Spain on a passport exactly as before. Watch for announcements; the EU has committed to giving several months' notice, plus a transitional period once it launches.

## FAQs

### Can my spouse work in Spain on a family permit?

Yes. Unlike the non-lucrative visa, the digital nomad authorisation permits accompanying family members to work in Spain. Spouses, registered partners, dependent children, and dependent ascendants can apply with you or join you later, and their permits align with yours.

### How long until I can apply for Spanish citizenship?

Most Americans need ten years of lawful residence. Citizens of Ibero-American countries, the Philippines, Andorra, Portugal, and Equatorial Guinea qualify after two. Your digital nomad years count in full toward both permanent residency at five years and naturalisation.

### Do I need to speak Spanish to get the visa?

No. Spain imposes no language test for the digital nomad visa. Citizenship applications later require the DELE A2 and CCSE exams.

### Can I travel elsewhere in Europe on this permit?

Yes. Your Spanish residence permit lets you move within the Schengen Area for up to 90 days in any 180-day period, on top of your residence in Spain. Long absences work against you, though — renewal requires at least six months per year on Spanish soil.

### Should I apply from the US or from inside Spain?

Both work. The consulate route gives certainty before you move but delivers only a one-year visa. The in-country UGE-CE route delivers a three-year authorisation, resolves faster, and defers your autónomo registration, but it requires you to travel first and file within your legal 90-day stay. Your employment structure, your family situation, and your timeline should decide it.

### What happens if my Certificate of Coverage gets refused?

You register with Spanish Social Security instead — either through your employer registering with the TGSS as a foreign employer, or by converting to a contractor relationship and joining RETA as autónomo. Neither route ends your application, but both change your tax and employment position, so decide with advice rather than under deadline pressure.
